When someone brings a new idea, the first reply sets expectations. The rule is to stay calm and neutral, and say
nothing you can't stand behind later. Make no commitment you can't guarantee, and no criticism you can't back up.

# What Shape Up says

In chapter 3, the default response to any new idea is "Interesting. Maybe some day.", which the book calls "a very
soft "no" that leaves all our options open."[^shapeup-ch3]

- **Too early to decide.** "It's too early to say "yes" or "no" on first contact."[^shapeup-ch3]
- **No backlog entry.** "We don't put it in a backlog." The idea gets space instead, so you learn whether it matters
  and what it would take.[^shapeup-ch3]
- **Saying yes to everything** leaves "a giant pile of work that only grows."[^shapeup-ch3]
- **Keep a cool manner.** "It's important to keep a cool manner and a bit of a poker face." Too much enthusiasm "can
  set expectations that this thing is going to happen".[^shapeup-ch3]

# The two mistakes

The book covers the first mistake. The second, and the table, are our own analysis.

| | Over-committing | Dismissing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sounds like | "Great idea, let's do it!" / "Should be easy." | "That won't work." / "We tried that." |
| What it does | Makes an implied promise before anyone has looked at the cost or priority | Ends the conversation, and stops people from bringing ideas |
| Why it's unsafe | You can't guarantee it yet: no appetite, no shaping, and other work competes | Usually a reaction, not evidence. The idea might be right, or a narrower version might be |
| Later cost | Disappointment and lost trust when it doesn't happen | A missed problem, and a reputation for shutting people down |

Both are early conclusions. A neutral reply keeps both doors open.

# How to respond

This is our own synthesis.

1. **Acknowledge it without judging.** "Interesting, thanks for bringing it."
2. **Ask about the problem, not the solution.** "What's going wrong today?" "Who hit this, and how often?" This also
   starts the problem narrowing in [shaping](/software-engineering/planning/shaping.md).
3. **Say honestly what happens next.** "I can't promise anything now; there are no plans for it this cycle." If you
   say you will think about it, do.
4. **Raise a concern only with its evidence.** Name the fact: "Last time we changed X, Y broke because of Z." If you
   don't have the evidence, ask it as a question: "How would this work with Z?"
5. **If the answer is really no, say no.** A soft no is for ideas that are still open, not a polite way to avoid a
   decision already made.

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "Yes, we'll add it." | "Interesting. I can't commit to it now, but tell me what problem it solves." |
| "That's a bad idea." | "What happens with Z in that case?" (or name the evidence) |
| "Put it in the backlog." | "Let's see if it keeps coming up. If it does, it's worth shaping." |

# Limits

This section is our own analysis.

- A neutral reply can sound evasive. Pair it with a real question so it doesn't read as a brush-off.
- Requests from a manager or customer may be mandates rather than ideas. Ask about priority instead of soft-noing.
- Incidents, security and compliance issues aren't raw ideas. Handle them through their own process.
